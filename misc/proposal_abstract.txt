This chapter presents a framework for the mathematical and 
computational modelling of morphosyntactic simplification
processes at both the individual and the population level. The central question that
will be asked is: what proportion of second-language (L2) 
learners must be present in a speech community for simplification
to occur, in the sense that over repeated speaker-to-speaker
interactions the morphosyntactically complex variant is lost
altogether? To answer this question, a model of language
change in structured populations is proposed, building on
foundational work in the variational learning paradigm.
The existence of a bifurcation in this model -- between a
phase in which simplification is impossible and a phase in
which simplification is necessary -- is discussed using
analytical mathematical arguments which employ a number
of simplifying assumptions. From here, we proceed to drop
the simplifying assumptions one by one, implementing the
model as an agent-based simulation. This allows the modelling
of increasingly complex properties of language learning and
population dynamics, such as age-dependent decaying learning
rates, irregular interaction networks, finite populations,
and inter-individual differences in the acquisition of
morphosyntactically complex material. This increased realism
allows us to ask to what extent the analytically derived
bifurcation threshold retains its validity as the complexity
of real-world language acquisition and linguistic interaction
is approached. The chapter also discusses concerns of
technical implementation detail, focusing on the best
programming practices to adopt in order to ensure performant
agent-based simulations, and introducing a purpose-built
software package, LearningAutomata.jl, in the process.
